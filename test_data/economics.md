# Economics

## Microeconomics Fundamentals

Microeconomics studies individual economic agents — consumers, firms, and markets — and how they make decisions under scarcity. The core problem of economics is allocating limited resources among unlimited wants.

**Supply and Demand:**
- Law of Demand: as price rises, quantity demanded falls (inverse relationship)
- Law of Supply: as price rises, quantity supplied rises (direct relationship)
- Equilibrium: where quantity supplied equals quantity demanded; market clears
- Surplus: quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded → price falls
- Shortage: quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied → price rises

**Elasticity:**
- Price Elasticity of Demand (PED) = % change in Qd / % change in P
- Elastic demand (|PED| > 1): consumers respond strongly to price changes (luxury goods)
- Inelastic demand (|PED| < 1): consumers respond weakly (necessities, insulin)
- Cross-price elasticity: measures demand sensitivity to price of another good
- Income elasticity: normal goods (positive), inferior goods (negative), luxury goods (>1)

**Consumer Theory:**
- Utility: satisfaction or benefit from consuming a good
- Marginal utility: additional utility from one more unit; diminishes with quantity
- Budget constraint: income = P1×Q1 + P2×Q2; shows affordable combinations
- Consumer optimum: maximize utility where MU1/P1 = MU2/P2

**Producer Theory:**
- Production function: Q = f(K, L) — output as function of capital and labor
- Short run: at least one input (capital) is fixed; long run: all inputs variable
- Marginal product: additional output from one more unit of an input
- Law of diminishing returns: adding variable input to fixed input eventually decreases marginal product
- Total Cost = Fixed Cost + Variable Cost
- Marginal Cost (MC) = change in TC / change in Q
- Average Total Cost (ATC) = TC / Q; U-shaped due to fixed and variable cost interaction

## Market Structures

**Perfect Competition:**
- Many buyers and sellers; identical products; free entry/exit; perfect information
- Firms are price takers: P = MR (marginal revenue)
- Profit maximization: produce where P = MC
- Long-run equilibrium: P = MC = ATC (zero economic profit)
- Efficient allocation of resources; consumer surplus maximized

**Monopoly:**
- Single seller with no close substitutes; high barriers to entry
- Price setter: firm faces downward-sloping demand curve
- MR < P because lowering price applies to all units sold
- Profit max: MR = MC; then find P from demand curve
- Deadweight loss: monopoly produces less at higher price than competitive market
- Natural monopoly: economies of scale make one large firm most efficient (utilities)

**Oligopoly:**
- Few large firms; interdependent decision-making; high barriers to entry
- Nash equilibrium: each firm's strategy is best response to others' strategies
- Prisoner's dilemma: firms collectively benefit from cooperation but individually defect
- Cartel: explicit agreement to fix prices/output; illegal in most countries (OPEC example)
- Game theory models strategic interactions between firms

**Monopolistic Competition:**
- Many firms selling differentiated products; free entry/exit
- Each firm has slight price-making ability due to differentiation
- Short-run profit possible; long-run zero profit as firms enter
- Examples: restaurants, clothing brands, hairdressers

## Macroeconomics

Macroeconomics examines the economy as a whole — GDP, inflation, unemployment, and economic growth.

**GDP (Gross Domestic Product):**
- Total market value of all final goods and services produced in a country in a year
- Expenditure approach: GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)
  - C = consumption, I = investment, G = government spending, X = exports, M = imports
- Income approach: GDP = wages + rent + interest + profit
- Nominal GDP: measured at current prices; Real GDP: adjusted for inflation
- GDP per capita = GDP / population; measures average standard of living

**Unemployment:**
- Frictional: between jobs; short-term, natural
- Structural: skills mismatch due to technological or industrial change
- Cyclical: caused by economic downturns (demand-deficient)
- Natural Rate of Unemployment (NRU): frictional + structural; ~4-5% in U.S.
- Full employment: cyclical unemployment = 0
- Labor force participation rate = (employed + unemployed) / working-age population

**Inflation:**
- General rise in price level over time; measured by CPI (Consumer Price Index)
- CPI = (cost of basket in current year / cost of basket in base year) × 100
- Demand-pull inflation: too much money chasing too few goods
- Cost-push inflation: rising production costs (wages, oil) push prices up
- Hyperinflation: extremely rapid inflation (Zimbabwe 2008, Weimar Germany 1923)
- Deflation: falling price levels; can worsen recessions (debt-deflation spiral)

**Business Cycle:**
- Expansion → Peak → Contraction (recession) → Trough → Recovery
- Recession: two consecutive quarters of negative real GDP growth
- Depression: severe, prolonged recession (Great Depression 1929–1939)
- Leading indicators: stock prices, building permits, consumer confidence
- Lagging indicators: unemployment rate, CPI, interest rates

## Fiscal Policy

Fiscal policy uses government spending and taxation to influence the economy.

**Expansionary Fiscal Policy (stimulus):**
- Increase government spending OR decrease taxes (or both)
- Increases aggregate demand; shifts AD curve rightward
- Used during recessions to boost output and employment
- May cause budget deficits and increase national debt

**Contractionary Fiscal Policy (austerity):**
- Decrease government spending OR increase taxes
- Reduces aggregate demand; combats inflation
- Used during overheating economy to cool price pressures

**Multiplier Effect:**
- An increase in government spending triggers rounds of spending
- Multiplier = 1 / (1 − MPC), where MPC = marginal propensity to consume
- If MPC = 0.8, multiplier = 5; $100B spending → $500B increase in GDP
- Crowding out: government borrowing raises interest rates, reducing private investment

**Automatic Stabilizers:**
- Built-in mechanisms that cushion economic fluctuations without new legislation
- Progressive taxes: tax burden falls in recession; welfare spending rises
- Unemployment benefits: increase during downturns, supporting consumer spending

## Monetary Policy

Central banks (Federal Reserve in U.S.) control money supply and interest rates.

**Tools of Monetary Policy:**
- Open Market Operations: buying/selling government bonds
  - Buy bonds → inject money → lower interest rates (expansionary)
  - Sell bonds → remove money → raise interest rates (contractionary)
- Reserve Requirement: fraction of deposits banks must hold; rarely changed
- Discount Rate: rate central bank charges commercial banks for loans
- Federal Funds Rate: target rate for overnight interbank lending; primary policy tool

**Quantity Theory of Money:**
- MV = PQ (Fisher equation)
- M = money supply, V = velocity of money, P = price level, Q = real output
- If V and Q are stable, doubling M doubles P → inflation

**Monetary Transmission Mechanism:**
- Lower interest rates → cheaper borrowing → more investment and consumption → higher AD → higher GDP and employment
- Time lags: monetary policy takes 6–18 months to fully affect the economy

**Inflation Targeting:**
- Fed's dual mandate: price stability (~2% inflation) and maximum employment
- Taylor Rule: suggests interest rate based on inflation gap and output gap
- Zero Lower Bound: nominal rates cannot go below 0%; requires unconventional policy (QE)

## International Trade

**Comparative Advantage:**
- A country should produce and export goods with lower opportunity cost
- Even if one country is absolutely better at everything, trade still benefits both
- Example: U.S. and Mexico can both gain by specializing based on relative efficiency

**Trade Barriers:**
- Tariff: tax on imports; raises domestic price; protects domestic industry; reduces efficiency
- Quota: limit on import quantity; similar effects to tariff
- Subsidy: government payment to domestic producers; lowers their costs
- WTO (World Trade Organization): promotes free trade, resolves trade disputes

**Balance of Payments:**
- Current Account: trade in goods/services + income transfers
- Capital Account: financial asset flows (foreign investment, loans)
- Current account deficit financed by capital account surplus

**Exchange Rates:**
- Appreciation: currency strengthens → exports more expensive, imports cheaper
- Depreciation: currency weakens → exports cheaper, imports more expensive
- Purchasing Power Parity (PPP): exchange rates adjust so same goods cost the same globally

## Economic Growth and Development

**Sources of Long-Run Growth:**
- Capital accumulation: investment in physical capital (machines, infrastructure)
- Human capital: education, training, health
- Technological progress: innovation, R&D
- Institutions: rule of law, property rights, stable government

**Solow Growth Model:**
- Economies converge to a steady state where investment = depreciation
- Long-run growth per capita driven only by technological progress
- Capital deepening: increasing K/L ratio; subject to diminishing returns

**Development Economics:**
- GDP per capita differences across countries explained by geography, institutions, culture
- Poverty trap: low income → low savings → low investment → low income
- Microfinance: small loans to poor entrepreneurs without collateral (Grameen Bank)
- Human Development Index (HDI): combines GDP per capita, life expectancy, education

**Inequality:**
- Gini coefficient: 0 = perfect equality, 1 = perfect inequality
- Lorenz curve: plots cumulative income share against cumulative population share
- Kuznets curve: inequality rises then falls as countries develop (inverted U)
