# Psychology

## Research Methods

Psychology uses the scientific method to study behavior and mental processes. A hypothesis is a testable prediction. An experiment manipulates an independent variable to observe its effect on a dependent variable. The control group receives no treatment; the experimental group does.

Random assignment minimizes confounding variables by distributing participant characteristics evenly. A double-blind study prevents both participants and researchers from knowing who received the treatment, eliminating bias. Correlation measures the strength and direction of a relationship between variables but does not establish causation.

## Biological Bases of Behavior

The neuron is the basic unit of the nervous system. Dendrites receive signals; the axon transmits them. Neurotransmitters cross the synapse to send signals between neurons. Dopamine is linked to reward and motivation. Serotonin regulates mood. Acetylcholine controls muscle movement and memory.

The central nervous system includes the brain and spinal cord. The peripheral nervous system connects the CNS to the rest of the body. The autonomic nervous system controls involuntary functions: the sympathetic division activates fight-or-flight responses; the parasympathetic restores calm.

Brain structures: the hippocampus is essential for forming new memories. The amygdala processes emotions, especially fear. The prefrontal cortex handles decision-making and impulse control. The cerebellum coordinates movement. The hypothalamus regulates hunger, thirst, and body temperature.

## Sensation and Perception

Sensation is the detection of physical stimuli by sensory receptors. Perception is the interpretation of those signals. Absolute threshold is the minimum stimulus intensity detectable 50% of the time. The just noticeable difference (JND) is the smallest change in stimulus detectable.

Gestalt principles describe how we organize visual information: figure-ground (separating objects from background), proximity (grouping nearby objects), similarity (grouping similar objects), continuity (perceiving smooth paths). Top-down processing uses prior knowledge to interpret stimuli; bottom-up processing builds perception from raw data.

## States of Consciousness

Consciousness is awareness of oneself and the environment. Sleep occurs in cycles of approximately 90 minutes. NREM sleep has stages 1-3 moving from light to deep sleep. REM sleep is associated with vivid dreaming and memory consolidation.

Hypnosis is a state of heightened suggestibility. Psychoactive drugs alter mood, perception, or consciousness. Stimulants (caffeine, cocaine) increase neural activity. Depressants (alcohol, opioids) slow it. Hallucinogens (LSD, psilocybin) distort perception.

## Learning

Classical conditioning (Pavlov): a neutral stimulus paired with an unconditioned stimulus becomes a conditioned stimulus that produces a conditioned response. Operant conditioning (Skinner): behavior is shaped by consequences. Positive reinforcement adds a reward; negative reinforcement removes an unpleasant stimulus. Punishment decreases behavior.

Observational learning (Bandura): people learn by watching others. Cognitive maps are mental representations of environments. Latent learning occurs without reinforcement and is demonstrated only when needed.

## Memory

Encoding converts information to a memory trace. Storage maintains the trace over time. Retrieval accesses stored information. Sensory memory holds information for fractions of a second. Short-term (working) memory holds about 7 ± 2 items for roughly 20-30 seconds. Long-term memory stores information indefinitely.

Explicit (declarative) memory stores facts and events consciously. Implicit memory stores skills and conditioned responses unconsciously. The serial position effect shows better recall for first (primacy) and last (recency) items in a list. Interference occurs when memories compete: proactive interference is when old memories disrupt new ones; retroactive is when new memories disrupt old ones.

## Development

Piaget's stages of cognitive development: sensorimotor (0-2 years, object permanence), preoperational (2-7 years, symbolic thinking, egocentrism), concrete operational (7-11 years, logical thinking), formal operational (12+, abstract reasoning).

Erikson's psychosocial stages describe development across the lifespan as a series of conflicts: trust vs. mistrust (infancy), autonomy vs. shame (toddler), initiative vs. guilt (preschool), industry vs. inferiority (school age), identity vs. role confusion (adolescence).

## Social Psychology

Attribution theory explains how we interpret the causes of behavior. The fundamental attribution error is over-attributing others' behavior to personality rather than situation. Conformity is adjusting behavior to match group norms (Asch's line experiments). Obedience is following authority figures (Milgram's shock experiments).

Cognitive dissonance occurs when beliefs and actions conflict, creating psychological discomfort that motivates attitude change. The bystander effect shows that individuals are less likely to help in emergencies when others are present. Social loafing refers to reduced effort when working in groups.
